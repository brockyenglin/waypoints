N°01 — Mule deer · Field notes

150 miles, twice a year.

Wyoming's Red Desert-to-Hoback herd rides the spring greenup from desert floor to high country — through a gap that cost $2.1 million to keep open.

Every spring, thousands of mule deer leave Wyoming's Red Desert and travel 150 miles north to the high country. They don't hurry. They surf the green wave, timing their climb so they arrive at each elevation just as new growth peaks. The whole migration funnels through a handful of bottlenecks — some only a few hundred meters wide — and collar data is what revealed them: the longest mule deer migration ever recorded hid in plain sight until 2013.

One bottleneck sat so close to development that keeping it open cost $2.1 million. Corridors like this now decide the future of Western mule deer, and the maps are how they get protected. The diamonds on the map above are the pinch points — hover them.

N°02 — American woodcock · Field notes

Gone by morning.

Woodcock migrate alone, at night, barely above the treetops. The three birds crossing the map fly routes modeled on real GPS telemetry.

Woodcock don't migrate in flocks. Each bird lifts off at dusk, alone, flies a few dark hours low over the trees, and drops into thick young forest before dawn. For most of history that made the migration invisible: the birds were here, then they weren't.

Since 2017, researchers have fitted hundreds of woodcock with GPS transmitters lighter than a nickel, and the route finally has a shape — hops of 20 to 150 miles, long stopovers in river-bottom cover, a chain of habitat running from Canada to the Gulf. Lose a link and the chain breaks. Hover any stopover on the map to see the date and the cover the bird chose.

N°03 — Passenger pigeon · Field notes

From billions to zero.

The most abundant bird in North America went extinct in 36 years. Run the century on the map's timeline and watch it happen.

In the early 1800s, passenger pigeons may have outnumbered every other bird in North America combined — flights that darkened the sky for three days, nestings that covered 850 square miles. Then the telegraph and the railroad found the colonies. The last great nesting, in 1878, was emptied by rail at roughly 50,000 birds a day.

By September 1914 the species was a single elderly female in a Cincinnati zoo cage — and then it was zero. There is no GPS track for a passenger pigeon. The points on the map are drawn from ledgers, newspapers, and testimony: the only flight path the species has left.

Coverage

Five ways to read the same country.

Five kinds of question we build maps for. Tracked movement and occurrence records are on the globe today; the rest is where this is headed. Same woods, same water — different questions.

01

Migration & Movement

Where animals go and when they move. On the globe today: four tracked routes — three woodcock fall migrations modeled on EWMRC GPS telemetry, and the Red Desert–Hoback mule deer corridor from published collar research. Every other species layer is occurrence density: where a species has been recorded, not the path it took.

02

Range & Distribution

Where a species holds, where it's thinning, and where the old maps have quietly stopped being true. Range edges are real lines, and they move.

03

Habitat & Conditions

Trout don't read calendars; they read water temperature. Stream gauges, snowpack, forest age, canopy density — the conditions that decide whether animals are there at all.

04

Historical & Archival

Century-old banding cards, market ledgers, township records. What the documents say was here, drawn where it happened.

05

Hyperlocal

Single watersheds, single forests, single seasons — the scale where a map stops being interesting and starts being useful.

Sources

Where the numbers come from.

Every layer on this site is drawn from public, citable data — the same sources researchers use. If a number is on the globe, you can trace it back to the agency that measured it.

On the globe now

GBIF
Three billion occurrence records, queryable live — every species density layer is drawn straight from its API.
NASA Earth Observations
The globe itself: Blue Marble surface, Black Marble night lights, and monthly MODIS environmental layers.
Eastern Woodcock Migration Research Cooperative
Satellite-tagged woodcock from New Brunswick to the Gulf since 2017 — the telemetry our three fall tracks are modeled on.
Schorger passenger pigeon database
622 georeferenced records of the passenger pigeon's decline, compiled from historical accounts.
Western Migrations / Wyoming Game & Fish
Corridor data from the researchers who proved a migration map could move policy.

What we build from next

Movebank
Animal tracking data from research teams worldwide — the raw material of the movement maps to come.
USGS Bird Banding Laboratory
Band recoveries going back a century — origin-to-destination pairs, one row at a time.
USGS National Water Information System
Stream gauges logging temperature and flow every 15 minutes, decade after decade.
eBird / Cornell Lab of Ornithology
Hundreds of millions of checklists — the backbone of modern range and abundance maps.
State fish & wildlife agencies
Harvest surveys by county, species, and season, across the Great Lakes states and beyond.
